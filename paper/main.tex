\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Inventory decisions depend on forecasts of distributions, not simply forecasts of means.
For a retail product, the stock needed to achieve a target service level (e.g., covering demand in 95\% of replenishment cycles) depends on the upper tail of demand until the next replenishment can arrive. We refer to this horizon as the \emph{protection interval}, which consists of the time until the next inventory review plus the replenishment lead time.
Underestimating that tail generates lost sales, while overestimating it ties up working capital.
Retailers therefore devote substantial effort to building and maintaining forecasting pipelines that convert sales histories and operational covariates into order-up-to levels.

Retail demand forecasting has progressed from statistical rules to task-specific machine-learning and deep-learning systems that capture nonlinear temporal patterns and operational covariates \citep{PetropoulosEtAl2022,Salinas2020DeepAR,LimEtAl2021}. Time series foundation models (TSFMs) extend this progression by pretraining on large and diverse collections of time series and transferring the learned temporal structure to new forecasting tasks \citep{Ansari2024Chronos,LagLlama2024,TimesFM2024}.
This pretraining offers two practical possibilities. A TSFM can be applied in zero-shot mode, without updating its parameters on target-domain data, or adapted through fine-tuning. Its probabilistic output can also provide the multiple demand quantiles needed for service-level decisions rather than only a point forecast. These capabilities make TSFMs attractive for large retail assortments, where building and maintaining a separate forecasting specification for every product is difficult.

Evidence on their operational value nevertheless remains limited. In a large-scale Alibaba study, fully fine-tuned Chronos-2 \citep{Ansari2025Chronos2} improves monthly point-forecast accuracy by 3.5--5.4\% relative to the firm's production forecasting system, while other TSFMs do not achieve the same gains \citep{SuiEtAl2026}. This result establishes that pretrained models can improve industrial retail forecasts. Whether these improvements translate into better inventory decisions, however, remains an open question.

That distinction matters for two reasons. First, aggregate forecast metrics average performance across the predictive distribution, whereas a high-service inventory policy depends disproportionately on upper demand quantiles. A model can therefore improve overall forecast accuracy yet raise inventory cost if its upper tail is poorly calibrated \citep{Syntetos2009}. Second, inventory value is path dependent, where an order changes the stock available in later periods, and subsequent orders respond to the resulting inventory and pipeline states. Forecast quality must therefore be evaluated through the policy it induces, not only at the prediction layer.

Retail transaction data create a further evaluation problem. When stock is unavailable, observed sales can fall below underlying demand, causing logged-sales evaluation to understate shortage costs. Because a lower-stocking policy incurs shortages over a wider range of demand, censoring can hide more of its shortage cost. Thus, even on the same logged-sales path, evaluation can be biased toward the lower-stocking policy, compressing or even reversing the true cost difference.

These considerations motivate two operational questions. First, when do the probabilistic forecasts produced by TSFMs yield lower inventory cost than benchmark forecasting methods, particularly at high service levels? Second, how does this advantage change over repeated replenishment cycles, and under what conditions can censored-sales evaluation distort or reverse a policy comparison?

We study these questions using two retail datasets with complementary demand environments. Our primary source is the offline-retail dataset documented by \citet{ZhaoEtAl2020}, which links transactions, inventory, replenishment, products, and in-store displays at a large Chinese supermarket.  In our analysis sample of 2{,}000 SKUs, 68\% of daily observations are zeros, and observed sales may be censored by inventory. The complementary OSA retail-store benchmark, released by Tredence \citep{TredenceOSAData2021}, contains 100 consumer-goods store--SKU series with uncensored demand and longer replenishment lead times.

We evaluate Amazon’s Chronos-2 \citep{Ansari2025Chronos2}, Google’s TimesFM-2.5 \citep{TimesFM2024}, and Salesforce’s Moirai-1.1 \citep{Moirai2024} against an empirical policy based on historical sales and representative machine-learning, deep-learning, and decision-focused methods.
% against empirical quantiles, gradient-boosted quantile regression \citep{KeEtAl2017}, task-specific deep forecasters including DeepAR and TFT \citep{Salinas2020DeepAR,LimEtAl2021}, and decision-focused newsvendor methods including ERM and DDM \citep{BanRudin2019,ShiChenDuenyas2016}.
Our forecast-to-decision pipeline converts daily forecast quantiles into approximate demand distributions over the protection interval and uses them to set target inventory levels. We evaluate each policy in two complementary ways: static newsvendor cost for one protection interval at a time, and closed-loop replay over multiple replenishment cycles, where each replenishment decision changes the inventory available for subsequent decisions.


Our first finding is that inventory value varies across TSFMs and depends on the accuracy of the upper demand quantiles used to set stock targets. Among methods using only SKU-level sales histories, Chronos-2 achieves the strongest probabilistic forecasts and the lowest static newsvendor cost, whereas TimesFM-2.5 and Moirai-1.1 do not consistently outperform the empirical policy. Forecast rankings alone are insufficient. Gradient-boosted quantile regression slightly improves aggregate quantile-forecast accuracy but raises inventory cost by overstating the upper quantiles that determine stock targets. At the prespecified 95\% and 98\% service targets, zero-shot Chronos-2 reduces static newsvendor cost by 12.21\% relative to the empirical policy.

Our second finding is that demand censoring materially changes the comparison between zero-shot Chronos-2 and the empirical policy. Here, unobserved unmet demand means customer demand that is not recorded as sales because inventory is unavailable. 
Under logged sales, Chronos-2's closed-loop cost advantage is 3.46\% and is not statistically distinguishable from zero. While adding a reconstruction of the unmet demand increases the point estimate to 11.12\%. The reconstructed demand is used only for evaluation, not for model training.
Our analytical results show when omitting this demand favors the lower-stocking policy and when the resulting bias is large enough to reverse the policy ranking. Simulations in which underlying demand is fully observed confirm that reversal is possible. In the retail application, censoring compresses but does not reverse the point-estimate ranking. 
As we gradually add reconstructed unmet demand, the closed-loop advantage becomes statistically detectable after adding approximately 30\% of the total reconstructed amount, or 14.6\% of logged protection-interval sales. This calculation describes the sensitivity of the policy comparison; it does not estimate actual unmet demand.

\subsection{Contributions}

This paper makes two contributions.

First, we provide a decision-layer benchmark for TSFMs. The framework maps probabilistic forecasts into protection-interval stock targets and compares the resulting policies both decision by decision and over multiple replenishment cycles. It thereby evaluates forecast accuracy and inventory cost within a common operational pipeline.

Second, we characterize how censored sales alter inventory-policy evaluation. We derive exact static and closed-loop ranking conditions, verify through simulations with fully observed demand that censoring can reverse a ranking, and quantify how the focal retail comparison changes as the assumed amount of unmet demand varies.

For managers, the results show that policy comparisons based only on logged sales may be incomplete when sales are limited by stock availability. Retailers could supplement sales with availability records and examine whether conclusions remain unchanged under plausible amounts of unmet demand. The paper uses reconstructed demand only to evaluate unchanged policies and does not test retraining forecasting models on reconstructed demand.

\section{Literature Review}\label{sec:lit}
% ============================================================

Our work sits at the intersection of three streams: data-driven inventory management, demand forecasting in operations, and pretrained foundation models for time series.
We position our contribution relative to each.

\subsection{Data-Driven Inventory Management}

The classical newsvendor problem admits a closed-form solution when the demand distribution is known: order up to the $\alpha$-quantile, where $\alpha = p/(p+h)$ is the critical ratio \citep{Porteus2002}.
In practice, the distribution is unknown and must be estimated from data, creating the \emph{data-driven newsvendor} problem.

Two paradigms have emerged.
The \emph{estimate-then-optimize} (ETO) approach first estimates the demand distribution and then solves the newsvendor problem using the estimated distribution.
\citet{Liyanage2005} show that the plug-in quantile estimator is asymptotically optimal but can exhibit significant finite-sample bias.
The \emph{predict-then-optimize} approach of \citet{BertsimasKallus2020} uses covariate information (features) to produce conditional demand distributions, and shows that machine learning methods (random forests, $k$-NN, kernel regression) can significantly improve upon unconditional estimates.
\citet{BanRudin2019} propose a ``big data'' newsvendor that directly minimizes the empirical newsvendor cost, bypassing the distribution-estimation step entirely.

Adjacent decision-aware approaches learn or combine predictions from target-domain data.
\citet{OroojlooyjadidEtAl2020} train a neural network to map features directly to newsvendor order quantities, while \citet{WangKangPetropoulos2024} construct probabilistic intermittent-demand forecast combinations using both forecast-accuracy and inventory-control objectives.
Our main comparison instead asks what pretrained history-only forecasts contribute within a common inventory pipeline.

A separate line of work addresses the inventory problem when demand observations are \emph{censored} by stockouts---the retailer observes $\min(D, S)$ rather than true demand $D$.
\citet{HuhRusmevichientong2009} develop an adaptive algorithm with $O(\sqrt{T})$ regret for the censored newsvendor under iid demand.
\citet{ShiChenDuenyas2016} propose a nonparametric gradient-descent method (DDM) that directly optimizes the base-stock level from censored data, achieving the same $O(1/\sqrt{T})$ regret rate without estimating the demand distribution.
Recent work develops complementary approaches to recovering or acting on demand beyond the observed sales boundary.
\citet{PedregalTrapero2024} formulate a Tobit innovations state-space model with time aggregation for settings in which the censoring threshold is observed and can vary over time.
\citet{WangEtAl2025FreshRetail} provide a stockout-annotated retail benchmark and a two-stage pipeline that reconstructs latent demand before forecasting.
\citet{Rodrigues2025DiffusionAware} combines a Tobit likelihood with graph diffusion to recover censored demand while representing transfers toward related products.
From a decision perspective, \citet{HssaineSinclair2024} characterize how the observable censoring boundary determines identifiability and minimax regret in the offline censored newsvendor.
\add{\citet{TangEtAl2025} combine survival analysis and doubly robust estimation to learn feature-based pricing policies from censored offline data.}
\citet{GundemQi2025} study dependent censored demand in a joint pricing--inventory problem and develop censored and pessimistic fitted-Q-iteration procedures for learning sequential policies from offline data.
Together, these studies emphasize that the information available about stock availability and unmet demand is part of the decision problem, rather than merely a data-cleaning detail.
\reduce{They are adjacent to the present study: these methods recover censored demand or learn sequential policies, while our fixed-policy design quantifies how the demand measure changes policy evaluation.}\add{This literature recovers demand or learns and optimizes decisions from censored data. Our fixed-policy design instead characterizes how the demand measure changes the comparison between candidate inventory policies.}

Our work contributes to this literature in two ways.
\reduce{First, we evaluate a \emph{pretrained model} as the forecasting component of an inventory policy and follow its performance from static loss into closed-loop replenishment.}\add{First, we evaluate a \emph{pretrained model} as the forecasting component of an inventory policy and follow its performance from static newsvendor cost into closed-loop replay cost.}
Second, holding forecast-induced policies fixed, we measure how replacing latent demand with logged sales changes their operational ranking and decompose the resulting difference into holding- and shortage-cost channels.

\subsection{Demand Forecasting in Operations}

The operations management literature has increasingly recognized that forecast accuracy and decision quality are distinct objectives.
\citet{Syntetos2009} demonstrate that forecast improvements do not always translate to inventory improvements for intermittent-demand items.
\citet{PetropoulosEtAl2022} provide a comprehensive review of forecasting methods and their operational implications, noting that the ``last mile'' from forecast to decision remains underdeveloped.

Machine learning methods---gradient-boosted trees \citep{ChenGuestrin2016}, autoregressive neural networks like DeepAR \citep{Salinas2020DeepAR}, attention-based architectures like the Temporal Fusion Transformer \citep{LimEtAl2021}, and deep state-space models \citep{RangapuramEtAl2018}---have shown strong performance on forecasting benchmarks, but their operational value is less clear.
\citet{GuoEtAl2025} introduce Bayesian ensembles of life-cycle forecasts for new-product demand, emphasizing the importance of distributional (quantile) forecasts for inventory decisions.

\reduce{Most recently, \citet{SuiEtAl2026} provide the first large-scale industrial evaluation of TSFMs for retail demand forecasting, showing that a fine-tuned Chronos-2 outperforms Alibaba's production N-BEATS system.}\add{Most recently, \citet{SuiEtAl2026} benchmark Chronos-2, Moirai-1.1-R, and TimesFM-2.5 on two large-scale Alibaba retail settings against its production forecasting system, which is built on variants of N-BEATS and N-BEATSx.}
Their work highlights the importance of covariate design (distilling promotional effects from a structural model) and identifies a ``bitter lesson'' for demand forecasting: scalable pretrained models may outperform hand-engineered systems.
Our work extends this insight from the prediction layer to the decision layer: we ask whether a TSFM's forecasting advantage translates into better inventory decisions under realistic cost structures.

A separate challenge in retail forecasting is the \emph{cold-start problem}: new products have no sales history, making statistical forecasting impossible.
Traditional approaches rely on analogous-product methods \citep{Wright1997} or expert judgment.
Our short-history experiment shows that pretrained TSFMs can produce useful distributional forecasts with substantially less local history than the full-history empirical benchmark.

\subsection{Foundation Models for Time Series}

Foundation models---large neural networks pretrained on broad data and applied to downstream tasks without task-specific training---have transformed natural language processing \citep{Devlin2019BERT,Brown2020GPT3} and computer vision \citep{Dosovitskiy2021ViT}.
Their extension to time series is recent.

\citet{Ansari2024Chronos} introduce Chronos, a family of language-model-based TSFMs that tokenize time series values into a discrete vocabulary and are pretrained on a large corpus of public and synthetic time series.
\citet{Ansari2025Chronos2} extend this to Chronos-2, which uses a native quantile-head architecture (directly outputting quantile values at predefined levels) and achieves state-of-the-art probabilistic forecasting performance across 27 benchmark datasets.
Other TSFMs include Lag-Llama \citep{LagLlama2024}, TimesFM \citep{TimesFM2024}, and Moirai \citep{Moirai2024}.
\citet{LiuEtAl2025Sundial} introduce Sundial, a generative probabilistic TSFM that uses flow matching to produce sample-based forecasts from continuous-valued time series.

Despite strong benchmark results, independent evidence on TSFMs in real-world operational settings remains limited.
\reduce{\citet{SuiEtAl2026} provide the first large-scale industrial evaluation of Chronos-2 on Alibaba's retail data, showing that a fine-tuned TSFM outperforms a production N-BEATS baseline by 3.5--5.4\% on forecasting accuracy.}\add{Within that benchmark, fully fine-tuned Chronos-2 improves monthly point-forecast accuracy ($1-\mathrm{WMAPE}$) relative to the production baseline by 3.5\% for Tmall Supermarket and 5.4\% for Tmall Global Direct Import, whereas fine-tuned Moirai-1.1-R and TimesFM-2.5 remain below the baseline on this metric.}
\reduce{Their work focuses on the prediction layer---point-forecast metrics (WMAPE)---and identifies covariate design as a key driver of TSFM performance.}\add{Their evidence concerns the prediction layer and identifies covariate design as a key driver of TSFM performance.}
\reduce{Our paper complements theirs by moving from forecasting accuracy to \emph{inventory decisions}: we embed TSFMs in a complete forecast-to-decision pipeline and evaluate static cost, closed-loop policy cost, and the effect of censored demand measurement.}\add{Our paper complements theirs by moving from forecasting accuracy to \emph{inventory decisions}: we embed TSFMs in a complete forecast-to-decision pipeline and evaluate static newsvendor cost, closed-loop replay cost, and the effect of censored demand measurement.}
Benchmark metrics (CRPS, MASE, WQL) aggregate over the entire predictive distribution, but inventory costs are driven by the upper tail.
A model that improves median accuracy but miscalibrates the 95th percentile may increase, not decrease, inventory cost.

\section{Problem Setting and Analytical Results}\label{sec:setting}
% ============================================================

We consider a retailer managing $N$ SKUs under periodic review with a base-stock (order-up-to) replenishment policy, a standard inventory-control rule \citep{Porteus2002}.
\reduce{Each SKU $i$ is reviewed every $R$ days with a replenishment lead time of $L_i$ days, yielding a protection interval of $\text{PI}_i = R + L_i$ days.}\add{Each SKU $i$ is reviewed every $R$ days with a deterministic replenishment lead time of $L_i$ days, held fixed across its orders, yielding a protection interval of $\text{PI}_i = R + L_i$ days.}
We use a lost-sales system, where unmet demand leaves the system rather than backlogging. On day $t$, units ordered earlier and scheduled to arrive that day are received first. Together with the on-hand inventory carried into the day, they form the available inventory $I^{\mathrm{avail}}_{it}$. Let $D_{it}$ denote underlying (true) demand, observed sales then are $Y_{it}=\min\{D_{it},I^{\mathrm{avail}}_{it}\}$, leaving $I^{\mathrm{end}}_{it}=(I^{\mathrm{avail}}_{it}-D_{it})^+$ remaining units and $\mathrm{Lost}_{it}=(D_{it}-I^{\mathrm{avail}}_{it})^+$ lost units. \subsection{Demand Model}

\reduce{Let $d_{i,t} \geq 0$ denote the daily demand for SKU $i$ on day $t$. The \emph{protection-interval demand} is the cumulative demand over the next $\text{PI}_i$ days:}\add{The \emph{protection-interval demand} is the cumulative underlying demand over the next $\text{PI}_i$ days:}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pi_demand}
  D_i^{\text{PI}} = \sum_{t=1}^{\text{PI}_i} D_{i,t}.
\end{equation}

We represent the demand distribution nonparametrically through the conditional cumulative distribution function $F_i(\cdot \mid \cF_t)$, where $\cF_t$ denotes the information set (sales history) available at the review point.
The key input to the inventory policy is the $\alpha$-quantile of protection-interval demand:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quantile}
  S_i^* = F_i^{-1}(\alpha \mid \cF_t) = \inf\{s \geq 0 : F_i(s \mid \cF_t) \geq \alpha\}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Cost Structure}

We consider two newsvendor cost structures.
In the \emph{derived-cost} setting, the holding cost per unit per period is $h_i = \kappa_h \cdot c_i / M$, where $c_i$ is the unit cost, $\kappa_h$ is the annual holding-cost rate, and $M$ is the number of periods per year.
The shortage cost is derived from the newsvendor critical ratio: $p_i = h_i \cdot \alpha / (1 - \alpha)$.
In the \emph{margin-cost} setting, $p_i = \max(\text{price}_i - c_i, 0)$ is the unit margin, and $\alpha_i = p_i / (p_i + h_i)$ is the implied service level.
For \reduce{Zhao}\add{the Chinese-supermarket dataset}, $M=12$, so $h_i$ is a per-unit \emph{monthly} holding rate.
In the daily closed-loop replay it multiplies mean daily ending inventory within the month, whereas $p_i$ multiplies the month's total lost units; using a daily inventory mean prevents calendar-month length from mechanically rescaling the holding term.

\subsection{Decision Layer}

Given a forecaster that produces a probabilistic prediction of daily demand, the \emph{decision layer} proceeds in three steps:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Quantile grid.}
  The forecaster outputs a grid of quantiles $\hat{q}_{i,t}(\tau)$ for each day $t \in \{1, \ldots, \text{PI}_i\}$ at predefined levels $\tau \in \{\tau_1, \ldots, \tau_K\}$.
  We repair the grid by clipping negative values and enforcing monotonicity: $\hat{q}_{i,t}(\tau_k) \leq \hat{q}_{i,t}(\tau_{k+1})$.

\item \textbf{PMF reconstruction and convolution.}
  We reconstruct a probability mass function (PMF) on integer support $\{0, 1, \ldots, v_{\max}\}$ from the quantile grid using a midpoint CDF estimator.
  The production cap is $v_{\max}=60$ units per day for \reduce{Zhao}\add{the Chinese-supermarket dataset} and $v_{\max}=300$ for OSA; convolution over $m$ days therefore has support $\{0,\ldots,mv_{\max}\}$.
  Probability above the highest supplied level $\tau=0.99$ is closed at each row's repaired $\hat q(0.99)$, and the numerical cap is selected by increasing $v_{\max}$ until arm-level effect directions and paired loss differences stabilize.
  We then convolve the $\text{PI}_i$ daily PMFs via the fast Fourier transform (FFT) to obtain the PMF of protection-interval demand:
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:convolve}
    p_i^{\text{PI}} = \text{IFFT}\!\left(\prod_{t=1}^{\text{PI}_i} \text{FFT}(p_{i,t})\right),
  \end{equation}
  where $p_{i,t}$ is the daily PMF for day $t$.
  This convolution treats daily demands as independent (conditional on history) but allows the marginal distributions to vary across days.
  Every forecasting arm uses this same aggregation rule, so differences in the resulting base-stock levels arise from the predicted daily marginals under a common dependence specification.

\item \textbf{Base-stock level.}
  We extract the base-stock level as $S_i = F_i^{-1}(\alpha)$ from the convolved PMF.
  We consider three policies:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item[\textbf{P1}:] $S_i = F_{R,i}^{-1}(\alpha)$, using only the review-period PMF (ignores lead time---a deliberate mismatch for comparison);
    \item[\textbf{P2}:] $S_i = m \cdot \mu_R + z_\alpha \sqrt{m} \cdot \sigma_R$, a normal approximation where $({\mu_R, \sigma_R})$ are the moments of review-period demand and $m = \text{PI}/R$;
    \item[\textbf{P3}:] $S_i = F_{\text{PI},i}^{-1}(\alpha)$, the numerically reconstructed protection-interval quantile.
  \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We evaluate each forecaster--policy pair on four metrics:

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Cycle service rate} (CSR): the fraction of replenishment cycles with no stockout, $\text{CSR} = |\{j : y_j \leq S_j\}| / n$.
\item \emph{Fill rate} (FR): the fraction of demand fulfilled from stock, $\text{FR} = 1 - \sum_j \max(y_j - S_j, 0) / \sum_j y_j$.
\item \reduce{\emph{Static protection-interval cost}}\add{\emph{Static newsvendor cost}}:
  \[
  C^{\mathrm{static}} = \sum_j \left[h_j (S_j-y_j)^+ + p_j (y_j-S_j)^+\right].
  \]
  This is exactly $(h_j+p_j)$ times the $\alpha$-pinball loss observation by observation when $\alpha=p_j/(p_j+h_j)$.
\item \emph{Closed-loop replay cost}:
  \[
  C^{\mathrm{replay}}_{im}
  =h_{im}\,\operatorname{mean}_{t\in m}(I^{\mathrm{end}}_{it})
   +p_{im}\sum_{t\in m}\mathrm{Lost}_{it}.
  \]
  Each arm places its own order $Q_{it}=(S_{it}-\mathrm{IP}_{it})^+$, schedules that order to arrive after $L$ days, and therefore generates its own on-hand and pipeline trajectory.
  After the replay origin, each arm's decisions use its own orders; commitments placed before the origin enter only as an arm-common initial pipeline.
\item \emph{Normalized pinball loss} (NPL): the pinball loss normalized by actual demand, aggregated across quantile levels.
\end{itemize}

On the \reduce{Zhao}\add{Chinese-supermarket} dataset, \reduce{observed sales satisfy $y_j=\min(D_j,A_j)$}\add{we write logged sales as $Y_j=\min(D_j,A_j)$ for the comparison below}, where $A_j$ is \reduce{available inventory}\add{the availability bound associated with static observation $j$; the daily replay instead uses the inventory-state notation in Table~\ref{tab:inventory-state-notation}}.
CSR and FR are therefore \emph{upper bounds} on the true service metrics.
We report this explicitly and validate conclusions on the uncensored OSA dataset.

\subsection{\add{Policy Evaluation under Censored Demand}}\label{sec:censoring-theory}

\add{Existing work learns decisions from censored observations or identifies policy value under censoring \citep{ShiChenDuenyas2016,TangEtAl2025}.}
\add{Our object differs: holding two candidate policies fixed, we characterize how replacing latent demand with logged sales changes their measured cost difference.}

\paragraph{\add{Static comparison.}}
\add{For a finite set of static cells $\mathcal J$, let $c_j(S,x)=h_j(S-x)^++p_j(x-S)^+$ and consider fixed targets $S_j^H\geq S_j^L$. Define the high-minus-low cost difference under demand measure $X\in\{Y,D\}$ as $\Delta_X^{\mathrm{st}}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal J}[c_j(S_j^H,X_j)-c_j(S_j^L,X_j)]$, where $Y$ is logged sales and $D$ is latent demand.}

\begin{proposition}[\add{Static policy-dependent censoring wedge}]\label{prop:censoring-wedge}
\add{Suppose $h_j,p_j\geq0$, $h_j+p_j>0$, $Y_j\leq D_j$, and $S_j^H\geq S_j^L$ for every cell $j$. Then}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:static-censoring-wedge}
\begin{aligned}
\add{\Delta_Y^{\mathrm{st}}-\Delta_D^{\mathrm{st}}}
&\add{=\sum_{j\in\mathcal J}(h_j+p_j)}\\
&\quad\add{\times\left[\min\{D_j,S_j^H\}-\max\{Y_j,S_j^L\}\right]^+}\\
&\add{\equiv W^{\mathrm{st}}\geq0.}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\add{The inequality is strict exactly when $(Y_j,D_j]$ and $(S_j^L,S_j^H]$ overlap with positive length for at least one cell. If the higher-stock policy is better under latent demand, $\Delta_D^{\mathrm{st}}<0$, censored-sales evaluation reverses the ranking exactly when $W^{\mathrm{st}}> -\Delta_D^{\mathrm{st}}$; equality produces a tie.}
\end{proposition}

\add{Thus censoring outside the interval between the targets does not affect their comparison; the relevant object is weighted overlap, not the censoring rate alone. If targets cross across cells, each overlap enters with the sign of the local target difference, so an average target ordering is insufficient.}

\paragraph{\add{Closed-loop comparison.}}
\begin{proposition}[\add{Closed-loop policy-dependent censoring bridge}]\label{prop:closed-loop-censoring}
\add{Fix two candidate policy rules $H$ and $L$, the replay protocol, initial inventory and pipeline states, and any exogenous randomization, holding each fixed across demand measures $X\in\{Y,D\}$. The induced state and order paths may differ with $X$. For policy $a\in\{H,L\}$, let the finite total replay cost be $C_a(X)=\mathcal H_a(X)+\mathcal P_a(X)$, where $\mathcal H_a(X)$ and $\mathcal P_a(X)$ are holding and shortage costs. Define $\Delta_X^{\mathrm{CL}}=C_H(X)-C_L(X)$, $G_a=\mathcal H_a(Y)-\mathcal H_a(D)$, and $U_a=\mathcal P_a(D)-\mathcal P_a(Y)$. Then, pathwise,}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:closed-loop-censoring-condition}
\begin{aligned}
\add{W^{\mathrm{CL}}}
&\add{\equiv\Delta_Y^{\mathrm{CL}}-\Delta_D^{\mathrm{CL}}}\\
&\add{=\bigl[C_H(Y)-C_L(Y)\bigr]-\bigl[C_H(D)-C_L(D)\bigr]}\\
&\add{=(U_L-U_H)+(G_H-G_L).}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\add{Logged sales shift the measured comparison toward policy $L$ if and only if $W^{\mathrm{CL}}>0$. If $H$ is less costly under latent demand, $\Delta_D^{\mathrm{CL}}<0$, the measured ranking reverses in favor of $L$ if and only if $W^{\mathrm{CL}}> -\Delta_D^{\mathrm{CL}}$; equality produces a tie.}
\end{proposition}

\reduce{Unlike Proposition~\ref{prop:censoring-wedge}, Proposition~\ref{prop:closed-loop-censoring} is an accounting identity and does not imply $W^{\mathrm{CL}}\geq0$: changing the demand path also changes policy-specific inventory and pipeline states. The identity applies to absolute cost differences; relative effects with changing denominators do not satisfy the same additive bridge. Electronic Companion Section~EC.4 proves both propositions, gives the signed-overlap extension, and reports known-truth validations.}\add{Unlike Proposition~\ref{prop:censoring-wedge}, Proposition~\ref{prop:closed-loop-censoring} is an accounting identity and does not imply $W^{\mathrm{CL}}\geq0$: changing the demand path also changes policy-specific inventory and pipeline states. The identity applies to absolute cost differences; relative effects with changing denominators do not satisfy the same additive bridge. Electronic Companion Section~EC.5 proves both propositions, gives the signed-overlap extension, and reports known-truth validations.}

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}
% ============================================================

This section describes the forecasting arms, fine-tuning procedure, and the baseline methods.

\subsection{TSFM: Chronos-2}\label{subsec:chronos}

We use Chronos-2 \citep{Ansari2025Chronos2}, a 120-million-parameter TSFM built on an encoder-only Transformer backbone with native support for covariates and cross-series learning.
The model is pretrained on over 100 billion time points from diverse domains (energy, finance, retail, weather) plus synthetic series generated via kernel-based Gaussian processes \citep{Ansari2024Chronos}.
Unlike the original Chronos, which tokenizes values into a discrete vocabulary, Chronos-2 uses a native quantile-head architecture that directly outputs quantile forecasts at 21 levels $\tau \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, \ldots, 0.90, 0.95, 0.99\}$ for each of the next $H$ days.
We also evaluate TimesFM-2.5 \citep{TimesFM2024} (200M parameters, patched-decoder architecture) and Moirai-1.1 \citep{Moirai2024} (311M parameters, masked-encoder architecture) in zero-shot mode for cross-TSFM comparison.
All three models receive the same SKU-level sales history and forecast horizon, and their probabilistic outputs pass through the same quantile-repair, PMF-reconstruction, and inventory-decision layer.
The comparison therefore benchmarks each model's deployable zero-shot interface under a common operational pipeline.
No feature engineering, covariate data, or task-specific training is required for any zero-shot TSFM evaluation.
All cross-architecture conclusions use only these zero-shot arms.
The Chronos-2 fine-tuning arms evaluate within-model adaptation and are not used to support claims of cross-architecture superiority.

We evaluate Chronos-2 in two modes:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Zero-shot} (chronos2-zs): the pretrained model is applied directly to each SKU's sales history with no adaptation.
\item \textbf{Fine-tuned} (chronos2-ft): the model is fine-tuned on the training portion of the target dataset using standard pinball loss across all 21 quantile levels, with early stopping on a held-out inner validation set.
\end{itemize}

On the \reduce{Zhao}\add{Chinese-supermarket} panel, the forecasting and fine-tuning arms learn from logged sales $Y$, which is the demand measure available to the retailer.
\reduce{The semi-synthetic latent-demand draws $D$ are introduced only in Section~\ref{sec:halfsynthetic}, after policy construction, to evaluate the same fixed policies under an alternative demand measure.}\add{The semi-synthetic latent-demand draws $D$ are constructed only in Section~\ref{sec:halfsynthetic}, after policy construction, to evaluate the same fixed policies under an alternative demand measure.}

\subsection{Baseline Forecasters}

\paragraph{Empirical quantiles (emp-daily).}
For each SKU, we compute the empirical quantiles of daily sales from the full available history at each level $\tau$.
This method uses no model and no features; it is the simplest reasonable baseline.
The same daily quantile grid is replicated for each forecast horizon day, then convolved as described in Section~\ref{sec:setting}.
In the rolling-origin policy analysis, we use a stronger adaptive benchmark: the empirical estimator is retuned at each origin using only preceding origins, and the selected specification is then held fixed for the scored comparison.

\paragraph{Gradient-boosted quantile regression (GBDT).}
We train a LightGBM \citep{KeEtAl2017} quantile regressor with 21 quantile levels.
We consider two feature sets: \emph{lean} (sales lags and rolling statistics only) and \emph{rich} (adding inventory position, purchase orders, lead times, and category dummies).
Training uses only data prior to the validation window.

\paragraph{DeepAR \citep{Salinas2020DeepAR}.}
DeepAR is a probabilistic autoregressive model based on a two-layer LSTM that produces forecast distributions by parameterizing the likelihood at each time step.
We train DeepAR with a multi-quantile loss at the same 21 quantile levels as Chronos-2, using the NeuralForecast implementation \citep{OlivaresEtAl2022}.
The model uses only lagged sales as input (no covariates), matching the information set of the zero-shot TSFM.
We include DeepAR as a representative of task-specific deep learning probabilistic forecasters.

\paragraph{Temporal Fusion Transformer \citep[TFT;][]{LimEtAl2021}.}
TFT combines multi-head attention with gating mechanisms and variable selection to produce multi-horizon quantile forecasts.
Like DeepAR, we train TFT with the same 21-level multi-quantile loss and restrict input to sales history only.
TFT represents the state of the art in task-specific Transformer forecasting models, providing a natural comparison point between purpose-built architectures and pretrained foundation models.

\paragraph{ERM newsvendor \citep{BanRudin2019}.}
The empirical risk minimization (ERM) approach bypasses distribution estimation and directly minimizes the newsvendor cost:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:erm}
  \min_{q(\cdot)} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[b \cdot (d_i - q(\mathbf{x}_i))^+ + h \cdot (q(\mathbf{x}_i) - d_i)^+\right],
\end{equation}
where $q(\mathbf{x})$ maps features to a stocking level.
This is equivalent to quantile regression at the critical ratio $\alpha = b/(b+h)$, but applied \emph{directly} to the protection-interval demand, skipping the daily forecast and convolution steps of our ETO pipeline.
We implement two variants: linear quantile regression (Ban \& Rudin's original formulation) and a nonlinear extension using LightGBM.
Both variants use the same rich feature set as GBDT (lagged sales, inventory positions, SKU attributes).
The ERM baseline tests whether end-to-end decision optimization outperforms the two-stage ETO approach.

\paragraph{DDM \citep{ShiChenDuenyas2016}.}
The data-driven method directly optimizes the base-stock level from censored sales data using stochastic gradient descent.
The update rule is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ddm}
  S_{t+1} = \left[S_t - \eta_t \left(h \cdot \ind[S_t > d_t] - p \cdot \ind[S_t \leq d_t]\right)\right]^+,
\end{equation}
where $d_t = \min(D_t, S_t)$ is the censored observation, $\eta_t = 1/\sqrt{t}$ is the learning rate, and $[\cdot]^+$ denotes projection onto $\R_+$.
DDM achieves $O(1/\sqrt{T})$ regret under iid demand.
We use it as an operational benchmark for how a censored-demand learning rule behaves in a nonstationary logged-sales environment.

\subsection{Operating-Point Fine-Tuning}\label{subsec:da_ft}

To test whether the loss function used during fine-tuning affects inventory decisions, we implement three variants by modifying the Chronos-2 loss computation:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Pinball} (standard): the quantile loss $\ell_\tau(y, \hat{q}) = \tau(y - \hat{q})^+ + (1-\tau)(\hat{q} - y)^+$ summed over all 21 native quantile levels.
\item \textbf{Operating-point}: the quantile loss computed only at the native level $\tau^* = \alpha$ (both $\alpha = 0.85$ and $\alpha = 0.95$ are natively trained levels, so no interpolation is required).
\item \textbf{$\alpha$-focused}: a Gaussian-weighted pinball loss centered at $\alpha$ with bandwidth $\sigma = 0.15$:
    \begin{align*} 
    \ell_{\text{f}} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k \cdot \ell_{\tau_k}(y, \hat{q}_{\tau_k}), \\
    w_k \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{(\tau_k - \alpha)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).
    \end{align*}
\end{enumerate}

All three variants are fine-tuned with identical hyperparameters (learning rate, epochs, batch size, early stopping) on the same training data.

\subsection{Prequential Protocol}

We follow a strict temporal separation to prevent information leakage:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Training window}: all data before the validation period.
  Fine-tuning and GBDT training use only this window.
\item \textbf{Validation window}: a held-out period used for model selection, hyperparameter tuning, and all results reported in the main text.
\item \textbf{Test window}: a final held-out period, frozen until all validation-window analysis is complete.
  Test results are reported without modification as a replication check.
\end{enumerate}

All design choices---forecasting arm, inventory policy, cost parameters---are fixed on the validation window.

\paragraph{Pretraining provenance.}
Chronos-2 combines public time-series corpora with synthetic series generated from kernel-based Gaussian processes \citep{Ansari2024Chronos}.
We evaluate the model through temporal out-of-sample prediction and decision-layer replication.
Every scored forecast uses information available before its decision date, and the second dataset supplies a distinct demand environment for assessing directional transfer.

\paragraph{Compute costs.}
All experiments run on a single Apple M2 Max (38 GPU cores, 64 GB unified memory).
Inference for 2{,}000 SKUs over one 63-day window takes approximately 40 seconds for Chronos-2 (MPS), 30 seconds for TimesFM-2.5, and 13 minutes for Moirai-1.1 (CPU, as MPS does not support float64).
Fine-tuning Chronos-2 for 500 steps takes $\sim$2 minutes per variant.
GBDT and ERM training are negligible ($<$5 seconds).
\reduce{The full static decision-layer evaluation (16 arms $\times$ 2 windows) completes in $\sim$13 minutes.}\add{The full static newsvendor evaluation across both windows completes in $\sim$13 minutes.}

\section{Where Chronos-2's Gains Are Largest}\label{sec:mechanism}
% ============================================================

The aggregate comparison masks substantial product-level heterogeneity.
This section documents where Chronos-2's point-estimate gains are largest by relating its SKU-level cost difference to changes in the local demand level.
We focus on the \reduce{Zhao}\add{Chinese-supermarket} dataset, where 2{,}000 SKUs observed over two validation months provide approximately 3{,}400 SKU-month comparisons.

\subsection{Demand-Level Shifts and Relative Performance}

For each SKU-month, we compute the \emph{level-shift ratio}: validation-period mean daily sales divided by the historical mean.
A ratio above one indicates an upward shift and a ratio below one indicates a downward shift.
We also compute three control variables: the coefficient of variation of a 30-day rolling mean (\emph{cv\_rolling}), the zero-demand share (\emph{zero\_share}), and the ADF test $p$-value for stationarity (\emph{adf\_p}).

Electronic Companion Figure~EC.2 reports Chronos-2's cost advantage by level-shift-ratio quintile.
The clearest advantage appears in the top quintile (Q5), which contains the largest upward shifts (ratio 1.67--44.0).
In Q5, Chronos-2 saves 13.3\% of total cost relative to the empirical baseline (\$15{,}400 in aggregate over two months).
Chronos-2 wins 34\% of individual SKU-month comparisons in Q5, compared with 18--28\% in Q1--Q4, and its Q5 wins are economically large.
This pattern is consistent with a simple operational advantage: a forecast based on recent temporal structure can respond more quickly than a full-history empirical distribution when demand shifts upward.

The Q3 aggregate is driven by three SKU-months with large upward forecast errors; excluding those observations reduces the quintile-level disadvantage to less than 5\%.
Together, Q3 and Q5 show that responsiveness has both value and risk: it creates large gains when a shift persists and large errors when a temporary increase is extrapolated.

\subsection{Cross-Sectional Association}

To disentangle drift from other demand characteristics, we estimate an OLS regression of the per-SKU-month cost difference (empirical minus TSFM) on the drift ratio and control variables:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ols_mechanism}
  \Delta\text{Cost}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{drift}_i + \beta_2 \cdot \text{cv}_i + \beta_3 \cdot \text{zero}_i + \varepsilon_i.
\end{equation}

The estimated coefficient on the level-shift ratio is $\hat\beta_1 = 3.17$ ($t = 3.8$): larger upward shifts are associated with a larger cost advantage for Chronos-2.
The coefficient of variation ($\hat\beta_2 = -3.49$) and zero share ($\hat\beta_3 = -22.38$) capture additional cross-sectional heterogeneity.
The regression explains 2.3\% of the SKU-month variation, supporting the level-shift ratio as a systematic correlate of relative performance.

\subsection{Operational Interpretation}

The heterogeneity analysis yields a useful implementation rule: Chronos-2 is especially valuable for SKUs undergoing upward demand-level shifts.
Full-history empirical quantiles pool observations across regimes and therefore adjust gradually, whereas Chronos-2 places greater weight on the temporal structure in its current context window.
The short-context experiment in Section~\ref{sec:ws6} provides complementary evidence: much of the model's performance is already present with 30 days of local history.

This finding connects to the theoretical literature on nonparametric inventory management.
The DDM algorithm of \citet{ShiChenDuenyas2016} provides an instructive comparison because its regret guarantee is derived under iid demand.
The mean level-shift ratio in our data is 0.878, and Section~\ref{sec:ws5} shows that the logged-data implementation carries substantially more stock than Chronos-2 in this changing-demand environment.

The same perspective helps explain the disconnect between prediction and decision layers observed for GBDT.
GBDT's quantile forecasts are well-calibrated on average (NPL comparable to the empirical method), but systematically over-estimate the upper tail, leading to over-stocking.
Chronos-2 produces tighter upper-tail forecasts that more closely track the current demand level in this sample.

\subsection{When Forecast Gains Become Inventory Gains}\label{sec:conversion}

The semi-synthetic experiment above uses the two prespecified high service levels ($\alpha\in\{0.95,0.98\}$) and a single contrast between censored sales ($Y$) and latent demand ($D$).
\reduce{A natural question is: \emph{at which service levels and censoring rates does a static forecasting advantage convert into a detectable inventory-cost reduction?}}\add{A natural question is: \emph{at which service levels and censoring rates does a static newsvendor cost advantage convert into a detectable closed-loop inventory-cost reduction?}}

\paragraph{Conversion condition.}
\reduce{Let $R_0(\alpha,\lambda)$ denote the ratio-of-sums static cost effect (Chronos-2 vs.\ retuned empirical) and $R_3(\alpha,\lambda)$ the corresponding dynamic replay cost effect, both evaluated on demand $X^{(k,\lambda)}$.}\add{Let $R_0(\alpha,\lambda)$ denote the ratio-of-sums static newsvendor cost effect (Chronos-2 vs.\ retuned empirical) and $R_3(\alpha,\lambda)$ the corresponding closed-loop replay cost effect, both evaluated on demand $X^{(k,\lambda)}$.}
Define the attenuation $A(\alpha,\lambda)=R_3-R_0$.
Then the exact decomposition
\begin{equation}\label{eq:conversion}
R_3(\alpha,\lambda)=R_0(\alpha,\lambda)+A(\alpha,\lambda)
\end{equation}
\reduce{implies that a static advantage ($R_0<0$) translates into a dynamic advantage ($R_3<0$) if and only if}\add{implies that a static newsvendor cost advantage ($R_0<0$) translates into a closed-loop replay cost advantage ($R_3<0$) if and only if}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:conversion_cond}
A(\alpha,\lambda)<-R_0(\alpha,\lambda).
\end{equation}
\reduce{That is, attenuation must not exceed the static advantage in magnitude.}\add{That is, attenuation must not exceed the static newsvendor cost advantage in magnitude.}
\reduce{Equations~\eqref{eq:conversion}--\eqref{eq:conversion_cond} provide an exact analytical accounting of conversion: dynamic value equals static value plus attenuation.}\add{Equations~\eqref{eq:conversion}--\eqref{eq:conversion_cond} provide an exact analytical accounting of conversion: the closed-loop replay cost effect equals the static newsvendor cost effect plus attenuation.}
The grid evaluates this accounting condition at 25 prespecified points using familywise simultaneous inference.

\paragraph{Grid design.}
We evaluate a prespecified $5\times 5$ grid:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\alpha &\in \{0.80,0.85,0.90,0.95,0.98\},\\
\lambda &\in \{0,0.25,0.50,0.75,1\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Each $\alpha$ receives its own policy quantile and cost weights $h=\kappa_h c/12$, $p=h\alpha/(1-\alpha)$; policies are fixed across the censoring dimension and across latent-demand draws.
The recensoring fraction $\lambda$ interpolates between latent demand ($\lambda=0$, corresponding to $D$) and observed sales ($\lambda=1$, corresponding to $Y$): starting from $D$, a fraction $\lambda$ of naturally-censored (series, origin) months have their demand replaced with $Y$, yielding intermediate demand paths $X^{(k,\lambda)}$.
Operationally, $\lambda$ is the share of identified stockout-month records treated as sales-only rather than demand-recovered observations, so the grid traces a nested decline in demand observability.
The censored months are allocated through SHA-256 orderings keyed by latent-demand draw, series, and origin.
Thus the two-way bootstrap propagates both SKU-sampling and recensoring-allocation uncertainty.
Censoring sets are nested within each draw: for $\lambda_1<\lambda_2$, every month recensored at $\lambda_1$ is also recensored at $\lambda_2$.
The natural censoring rate is 35.3\% over the full eligible panel; restricting to the scored origins yields 36.5\% at $\lambda=1$.
Actual scored-origin recensoring rates (averaged across 50 draws) are $0\%$, $9.1\%$, $18.3\%$, $27.3\%$, and $36.5\%$ for $\lambda=0,0.25,0.50,0.75,1$.

Inference uses max-$|t|$ simultaneous bands---the maximum absolute standardized bootstrap deviation---over two families: a 50-dimensional \emph{conversion family} (25 $R_0$ + 25 $R_3$ values) and a 45-dimensional \emph{mechanism family} (25 $A$ values + 20 non-mechanical $\mathrm{DiD}$ values, excluding the five $\mathrm{DiD}(\alpha,0)\equiv 0$).
The resulting 95\% bands control the familywise error rate at 5\% within each prespecified family.
We define the conversion region as the set of grid points where the simultaneous upper bounds of \emph{both} $R_0$ and $R_3$ are below zero.
The analysis uses 50{,}000 two-way bootstrap resamples over $1{,}135$ SKU clusters and 50 latent-demand draws.
Within each replication, every grid estimand uses the same resampled SKU clusters, and every $D$-dependent estimand also uses the same resampled draw IDs; these shared picks preserve dependence across grid points and contrasts.
Attenuation and DiD are formed inside each replication before their bootstrap variances are estimated, retaining the covariance among their component estimands.
For each coordinate, we center the bootstrap deviations at the point estimate and studentize by its bootstrap standard error.
The 95th percentile of the replication-level maximum $|t|$ statistic supplies the family-specific simultaneous critical value.
The online appendix reports pointwise and simultaneous intervals for all 25 grid points.

The simultaneously supported set expands as the service target moves deeper into the tail: at $\alpha=0.95$, the conversion criterion is met only under latent demand, whereas at $\alpha=0.98$ it remains met through $\lambda=0.50$.
Point estimates favor Chronos-2 at 11 of the 25 grid points; four satisfy the predeclared simultaneous conversion criterion.

The monotonic structure provides strong descriptive evidence.
For all five service levels, $R_3$ worsens strictly as $\lambda$ increases (more censoring).
For all five censoring levels, $R_3$ improves strictly as $\alpha$ increases.
These patterns are consistent with the conversion condition~\eqref{eq:conversion_cond}: higher $\alpha$ enlarges Chronos-2's tail advantage ($R_0$ more negative), while higher recensoring is associated with greater attenuation, consistent with the pooled bridge decomposition, in which the differential evaluation distortion operates primarily through shortage cost.

\paragraph{Mechanism evidence at the grid level.}
Individual DiD values across the grid are uniformly negative in the point estimates; 16 of the 20 non-mechanical-zero entries have simultaneous intervals that exclude zero, with the four non-significant entries all at $\alpha=0.98$.
The pooled high-$\alpha$ DiD of $-8.28$ percentage points ($p<0.001$) provides the most precise demand-measure contrast.
\reduce{The grid complements that estimate by showing \emph{where} the resulting static advantage is large enough to convert into detectable inventory value.}\add{The grid complements that estimate by showing \emph{where} the resulting static newsvendor cost advantage is large enough to convert into detectable inventory value.}

\subsection{Complementary Implementation Evidence}

\paragraph{Operating-point fine-tuning.}\label{sec:ws3}
We compare standard multi-quantile pinball, operating-point pinball, and $\alpha$-focused pinball at three lead times.
Electronic Companion Table~EC.6 shows that fine-tuning improves on zero-shot performance throughout and that the operating-point loss delivers the lowest cost: 3.1\%, 4.2\%, and 5.2\% below standard pinball at $L=1,7,$ and $14$, respectively.
The critical ratio is unchanged by $L$; the growing difference reflects the aggregation of daily distributional errors over a longer protection interval.

\paragraph{Short-history performance.}\label{sec:ws6}
We next vary Chronos-2's local context from 7 days to the full history.
Thirty days capture most of the observed gain: NPL falls from 0.320 at 14 days to 0.271 at 30 days and then approaches 0.262 with full history.
The 30-day policy also improves on the full-history empirical benchmark (cost 94.20 vs.\ 101.37), showing that pretraining provides useful structure when local observations are limited (Electronic Companion Table~EC.7).

\paragraph{Censored-demand learning benchmark.}\label{sec:ws5}
Finally, we compare Chronos-2 with DDM \citep{ShiChenDuenyas2016}, an algorithm that updates target stock from censored observations.
In the nonstationary logged panel, DDM achieves the highest service rate but carries a median target stock of 60.5, compared with 19.5 for Chronos-2.
It incurs 70--92\% more cost (Electronic Companion Table~EC.8).
The comparison highlights a practical distinction: learning from censored observations and adapting to changes in the local demand level are separate capabilities.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

This paper studies the operational value of time series foundation models in inventory management.
\reduce{We embed three TSFMs in a forecast-to-decision pipeline, compare them with statistical, machine-learning, deep-learning, and decision-focused baselines, and follow their forecasts from static newsvendor loss into arm-specific closed-loop replenishment.}\add{We embed three TSFMs in a forecast-to-decision pipeline, compare them with statistical, machine-learning, deep-learning, and decision-focused baselines, and follow their forecasts from static newsvendor cost into closed-loop replay cost.}
The analysis connects probabilistic forecasting, inventory dynamics, and the measurement of unmet demand.

\reduce{Three main findings emerge.}\add{Two main findings emerge.}
First, TSFM value is model-specific and concentrated in decision-relevant upper quantiles.
\reduce{Chronos-2 produces the strongest probabilistic forecasts and the lowest static cost among the tested history-only methods, whereas TimesFM-2.5 and Moirai-1.1 do not consistently improve on the empirical benchmark.}\add{Chronos-2 produces the strongest probabilistic forecasts and the lowest static newsvendor cost among the tested history-only methods, whereas TimesFM-2.5 and Moirai-1.1 do not consistently improve on the empirical benchmark.}
\reduce{At the high service levels used in the rolling-origin analysis, zero-shot Chronos-2 reduces static cost by 12.21\% relative to a retuned empirical policy.}\add{At the high service levels used in the rolling-origin analysis, zero-shot Chronos-2 reduces static newsvendor cost by 12.21\% relative to a retuned empirical policy.}
The comparison also demonstrates why decision-layer evaluation matters: improvements in aggregate pinball loss need not produce better stocking levels when upper-tail calibration moves in the wrong direction.

Second, censored-sales evaluation creates an economically important wedge between forecast value and measured policy value.
\reduce{The 12.21\% static advantage becomes 3.46\% in the continuous observed-sales replay, an attenuation of 8.75 percentage points.}\add{The 12.21\% static newsvendor cost advantage becomes a 3.46\% closed-loop replay cost advantage in the continuous observed-sales replay, an attenuation of 8.75 percentage points.}
\reduce{When the same fixed policies are evaluated on semi-synthetic latent demand, the dynamic advantage is 11.12\% and attenuation is 0.47 percentage points.}\add{When the same fixed policies are evaluated on semi-synthetic latent demand, the closed-loop replay advantage is 11.12\% and attenuation is 0.47 percentage points.}
The difference-in-differences estimate is $-8.28$ percentage points.
\add{In the fixed-policy scaling sensitivity, the closed-loop advantage becomes statistically detectable after adding approximately 30\% of the calibrated hidden-demand increment, equivalent to 14.6\% of logged protection-interval sales.}
The DiD remains negative and statistically distinguishable from zero under Poisson and negative-binomial latent-demand generators ($-2.54$ and $-6.03$ percentage points), showing that the evaluation wedge persists across lighter and heavier reconstructions of unmet demand.
Within the calibrated factorial, the cost bridge attributes 98\% of the differential bias to shortage-cost measurement.
Logged sales lower measured shortage cost for both policies.
They favor the lower-stocking empirical policy more, thereby compressing Chronos-2's apparent advantage.
\add{The two propositions characterize the static and closed-loop wedges and give directly verifiable ranking-reversal thresholds. Known-truth simulations cross those thresholds; in the retail application itself, the point-estimate ordering is compressed but not reversed.}
Alternative quantile-to-PMF mappings leave attenuation nearly unchanged, while positive within-protection-interval dependence increases it from 8.75 to 9.56--11.70 percentage points.
At both operational service levels, Chronos-2 also attains higher realized coverage and smaller demand exceedances than the empirical policy under $Y$ and $D$.

\reduce{Third, measured conversion varies systematically with service level and demand-measurement quality in the calibrated grid.}\add{The service-level--recensoring grid further shows that measured conversion varies systematically with service level and demand-measurement quality.}
\reduce{The $5\times5$ analysis provides simultaneous support for both a static and a dynamic advantage at four prespecified combinations.}\add{The $5\times5$ analysis provides simultaneous support for both a static newsvendor cost advantage and a closed-loop replay cost advantage at four prespecified combinations.}
\reduce{Dynamic performance}
\add{Closed-loop replay performance} improves at higher service levels and deteriorates as a larger share of latent demand is replaced by censored sales.
The grid provides evidence for prioritizing deployment and improved measurement of unmet demand among the evaluated operating points.

The complementary analyses point to practical implementation choices.
Chronos-2 creates its largest product-level gains after upward demand-level shifts and produces useful decisions with relatively short local histories.
Operating-point fine-tuning improves cost by 3--5\% relative to standard multi-quantile fine-tuning in the examined settings.
\reduce{Under observed-margin costs and SKU-specific critical ratios, zero-shot Chronos-2 retains a 7.1--8.9\% static cost advantage across the examined holding-cost rates.}\add{Under observed-margin costs and SKU-specific critical ratios, zero-shot Chronos-2 retains a 7.1--8.9\% static newsvendor cost advantage across the examined holding-cost rates.}
\paragraph{Managerial Implications.}
Managers should match TSFM deployment to the operating environment.
High-service applications place greater weight on the upper tail, where Chronos-2 provides its clearest advantage.
Products experiencing upward demand shifts are attractive pilot candidates, and short-history performance makes the model useful for recently introduced or sparsely observed items.
\reduce{At the same time, firms that evaluate policies from transaction logs should invest in recovering lost demand through stockout flags, availability records, substitution data, or calibrated demand models.}\add{At the same time, firms that evaluate policies from transaction logs can supplement sales with stockout flags or availability records and assess robustness to calibrated latent-demand models.}
\reduce{Recovering unmet demand prevents a superior policy from appearing less valuable merely because observed sales omit the shortage events that distinguish policies.}\add{These steps can reduce the risk that a superior policy appears less valuable merely because observed sales omit the shortage events that distinguish policies.}
\reduce{Once deployed, retailers can monitor upper-tail coverage and stockout incidence by service class and use persistent deviations to trigger empirical-baseline retuning or policy review.}\add{Once deployed, retailers could monitor upper-tail coverage and stockout incidence by service class; persistent deviations may warrant empirical-baseline retuning or policy review.}

Formal \reduce{dynamic}\add{closed-loop replay} and semi-synthetic inference comes from the 1{,}135-series \reduce{Zhao}\add{Chinese-supermarket} common-support panel; the OSA analysis supplies a directional consumer-goods check under uncensored demand and longer lead times.
\add{The transaction records do not identify a finite upper bound on unmet demand, so the hidden-demand scaling exercise is a sensitivity analysis rather than an estimate of latent-demand prevalence.}
\reduce{Within this empirical scope, the fixed-policy factorial, exact cost bridge, alternative demand generators, and service-level--recensoring grid jointly establish how predictive quality, inventory dynamics, and demand measurement shape the measured operational value of Chronos-2.}
